How to use it, and what the numbers mean

Everything the app measures, how to get a reading you can trust, and how to tell a healthy turntable from one that needs attention.

01 Quick start

  1. Put a record on the platter so the phone lies on vinyl rather than on bare metal or the ribs of a mat. Anywhere on the record is fine.
  2. With the platter stopped and the phone lying still on it, press Zero sensor and wait two seconds.
  3. Press Start, then start the turntable. The first few seconds of spin-up are ignored automatically.
  4. Leave it running for at least a minute. Speed settles in seconds; wow and flutter need revolutions before they mean anything.
  5. Stop the platter. The reading freezes, and Save run files it in History.

Placement genuinely does not matter. A gyroscope measures how fast the whole platter turns, not how fast the phone travels, so off-centre reads exactly the same as centred. What matters is that the phone lies flat and cannot slide.

02 Getting a reading you can trust

Zero the sensor every session

A phone's gyroscope has a small standing offset that changes with temperature and with time since the phone was last woken. Left uncorrected it is the largest single error in the measurement — bigger than anything your turntable is doing wrong. Zeroing takes two seconds and removes it.

Zeroing also measures your phone's noise floor, printed under the buttons. Any wow and flutter figure near that number is the phone talking, not the deck.

Let it run

Thirty seconds gives a solid average speed. Wow is a slow wavering that can take several revolutions to come round, and the once-per-revolution figure needs a good number of turns before it separates from noise — a full side is not overkill.

Things that quietly ruin a reading

  • A sliding phone. On a slick mat a phone can creep, and creeping is rotation the app will faithfully report. A record under it usually solves this.
  • Holding or touching the phone while it measures.
  • A thick or uneven case that leaves the phone rocking rather than lying flat.
  • Backgrounding the app. iOS stops delivering sensor data; the app leaves a gap rather than reporting a false slow patch, but you lose that stretch of the run.
  • An unlevel turntable. Not an error in the measurement, but it is a real cause of speed variation worth ruling out first.

03 Reading the numbers

The big number

While the platter turns this is the live speed, damped slightly so it is readable. When you stop, it switches to the average for the run — that is the figure to quote and the one to trust. The average is the total angle the platter turned divided by the time actually measured, so a brief wobble cannot skew it.

Percentage — how far off target

The error against the nominal speed. The app picks the target automatically from what it sees; tap a chip to lock it if you want.

ReadingWhat it means
under ±0.1% Excellent. A well-adjusted direct drive lives here, and it is inaudible.
±0.1 – 0.3% Healthy. Typical of a good belt drive in decent order. Nobody hears this.
±0.3 – 0.8% Off, and worth correcting. Approaching the range where piano and sustained notes sound faintly wrong against a reference.
over ±1% Audibly off pitch to most listeners in an A/B. Something needs adjusting — see faults.

The green band on the meter and the graph is the “on speed” threshold from Settings, so you can set your own standard.

Cents — the same error, as pitch

A cent is a hundredth of a semitone, which makes speed error audible rather than abstract. Speed and pitch move together: run 1% fast and everything plays 1% sharp.

Speed errorPitch errorIn practice
0.1%1.7 centsInaudible.
0.3%5.2 centsAt the edge of what a trained ear catches in direct comparison.
0.5%8.6 centsNoticeable to some against a reference; rarely noticed alone.
1.0%17.2 centsMost people hear this when they have something to compare against.
5.9%100 centsA full semitone.

These are rough thresholds. Steady pitch error is far harder to notice than wavering pitch error, which is why a deck can be 0.5% fast and sound fine while a deck with real wow does not.

Wow & flutter — peak and RMS

Not how far from the target, but how much the speed moves around its own average. Peak is the largest departure in the run; RMS is the typical one. A deck can sit dead on 33.33 and still be unpleasant if these are high.

Do not compare these with a manufacturer's spec sheet. Published figures are weighted with a DIN/IEC filter that deliberately de-emphasises the frequencies the ear cares least about, and they are measured from a 3,150 Hz test record. These are unweighted and measured from a gyroscope, so they read higher — often several times higher — for the same turntable. That is the measurement differing, not your deck being bad.

Use them relatively, which is where they are genuinely strong:

  • Against your noise floor. Within about twice it, you are measuring the phone.
  • Against the same deck last month, or before and after a new belt.
  • Against another deck measured the same way, on the same phone.

Two runs on the same turntable should land close to each other. If they do not, something in the setup is moving — start with the phone sliding.

The speed graph

Deviation from the target over time, smoothed. The dashed line is the run's own average, the shaded band is your “on speed” threshold. Shape tells you more than height: a slow sine wave is wow, a steady climb or fall is drift, and a single spike is usually something bumping the table.

04 The platter map

The same error, but arranged by where the platter was in its rotation rather than by time. Every revolution is folded onto one circle and averaged, so anything that happens at a consistent point in the turn adds up while everything random cancels out.

Blue is slow, red is fast, green is within your threshold. The number in the middle is the strength of the once-per-revolution component.

What you seeWhat it suggests
Evenly greenNothing is happening at a fixed point in the rotation. This is the good result — whatever variation exists is not tied to the platter turning.
One blue arc, one red arc, opposite each otherA classic once-per-revolution pattern: the platter speeds up on one half and slows on the other. Suspect the belt joint, an eccentric pulley or spindle, or an off-centre platter.
A single narrow spikeSomething catching at one spot — a flat on the belt, debris in the bearing, or a rubbing plinth.
A busy, changing patternUsually just too few revolutions. Run it longer before reading anything into it.

Note that a belt drive turns its pulley many times per platter revolution, so a pulley fault often shows up in wow and flutter without leaving a clean once-per-revolution signature.

05 Diagnosing faults

SymptomLikely cause
Consistently fast or slow, rock steady A speed offset, not an instability. On decks with a trim, adjust it. On a belt drive with no adjustment, a worn belt or the wrong belt size is the usual culprit; on 45 check the pulley step or the speed selector is fully engaged.
Slowly drifts over several minutes Belt warming and stretching as it runs, or motor thermal drift. Compare a cold run with one after twenty minutes — see tests.
Clear once-per-revolution pattern Belt joint, eccentric pulley, bent spindle, or an off-centre platter. Rotating the belt on the pulley and re-measuring is a cheap test.
High wow with a clean map Something varying that is not locked to the platter: a tired belt, a slipping pulley, a dry bearing, or an unstable motor supply.
Speed drops when the stylus goes down Stylus drag overcoming the motor. Normal in tiny amounts; a large drop points to a weak belt, low torque, or a bearing that needs attention.
Wild or impossible numbers Almost always the phone, not the deck: it slid, it was not zeroed, or it was picked up mid-run. Re-zero and repeat.
Two runs disagree Trust neither. Re-zero, make sure the phone cannot creep, and run both again for longer.

Before changing anything mechanical, level the turntable and check the belt is clean. Both are free, both are common causes, and both change the numbers.

06 Tests worth running

  • Repeatability. Two runs back to back, nothing touched. The averages should agree closely. If they do not, fix the setup before believing anything else.
  • Stylus drag. One run with the arm parked, one with the stylus in a groove. The difference is how much your cartridge loads the motor — one of the more interesting things this app can show you, and invisible to a strobe disc.
  • Cold versus warm. A run from a cold start, another after twenty minutes. Belt drives commonly move a little as they warm.
  • Before and after a new belt. Save both runs to History; the comparison is the clearest evidence you will get of whether the old belt was actually tired.
  • 33 against 45. Both speeds should be off by a similar percentage. If only one is wrong, the fault is in the speed change mechanism rather than the motor.
  • Outer versus inner. Same run, phone near the rim and then near the label. They should read identically — a useful sanity check that the app is behaving.

History holds your saved runs and exports them as CSV. Everything stays on the phone.

08 What this cannot do

iOS delivers roughly 60 gyroscope readings a second. That is plenty for speed accuracy and for wow — the slow wavering, below a few hertz — but anything varying faster than about 30 times a second cannot be seen at all. That is the flutter end of the band a 3,150 Hz test record measures, and no phone app reading a gyroscope can reach it.

So: treat average speed as a real measurement, good to a few thousandths of an RPM over a minute. Treat wow and flutter as a wow measurement — excellent for comparing decks, belts and before-and-afters, not a substitute for a test record and a lab analyser, and not comparable with a weighted spec.

The phone is part of the measurement. Zero it, note the noise floor, and be suspicious of anything close to it.
